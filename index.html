Workshop Purpose

What We're Here to Do

Not a presentation about UX. An experience of design thinking — through building, storytelling, and shared discovery.

Five Goals

  • Build shared understanding of what UX Design can contribute to ISTS
  • Surface the most meaningful user and organizational challenges
  • Bring business stakeholders and designers into the same problem space
  • Identify where design can drive measurable impact across the full range of ISTS services, tools, and experiences
  • Leave with a small number of opportunity areas worth pursuing

Facilitator's North Star

The goal is not to teach UX. It is to let stakeholders experience design thinking by doing it.

The recurring question for both days:
Every time someone describes a user problem, the room asks: "How do we know this is true?" — and places it into one of three buckets.
We Know

Evidence exists — data, research, direct user feedback

We Believe

Strong assumption — not yet validated by evidence

We Don't Know

Genuine gap — research needed before acting

Hybrid Format

Running with Virtual Attendees

Some participants will attend remotely. This section covers what stays the same, what changes, and how to keep both groups working on the same problem in parallel.

What Stays the Same

  • All Day 2 sessions — Research Share-Out, What Is the City Being Asked to Fix?, Build the City You Want Users to Live In, Where the City Is Falling Short, What Does the City Need to Get Right for Users by 2027?, What the City Commits to Change
  • The evidence framework — virtual attendees apply it identically
  • Group assignments — virtual attendees are assigned to working groups and participate as peers
  • Dot voting and prioritization — done on the shared Miro board
  • All four output artifacts

What Changes

  • LEGO builds → Miro LEGO board (virtual attendees build digitally in parallel)
  • Architecture tour → virtual attendees join at 12:30pm; receive photo summary at lunch
  • Physical share-outs → room camera on LEGO tables; virtual attendees screen-share Miro builds
  • Design Studio share-out → virtual attendees view group LEGO models via room camera; add reactions and questions in Miro
  • Physical dot votes → Miro voting tool for virtual attendees simultaneously

Virtual LEGO — Miro Board

A Miro board version of LEGO Serious Play has been created for virtual attendees. They build using a digital brick library in parallel with in-room physical builds. Each workshop session has a dedicated Miro frame matching the physical prompt.

Before the workshop

  • Share the Miro board link with virtual attendees at least 3 days in advance
  • Ask them to open the board and verify edit access before Day 1
  • Send a 2-minute Miro orientation: frame navigation, how to add and move bricks, how to add sticky notes

During sessions

  • Virtual attendees build in their own labeled section of each frame
  • During share-outs, ask virtual attendees to screen-share their Miro section
  • At the end of each physical build phase, photograph each table's model and drop it into the corresponding Miro frame before share-outs begin
  • Group merge activities: virtual attendees move their Miro pieces into the shared group frame
Sync rule: Before every share-out, pause 60 seconds to upload photos of physical builds into Miro. This gives virtual attendees the same visual context as the people in the room — both groups should be looking at the same thing when someone is talking.

Room A/V Requirements

A/V is load-bearing for this workshop. Budget time to test it thoroughly the day before. A bad audio setup will effectively exclude virtual attendees from discussions even when they are technically connected.

Camera
Dedicated wide-angle camera (not a laptop webcam) on a tripod, pointed at the LEGO tables. Virtual attendees need to clearly see physical builds. Reposition between sessions if needed.
Room microphone
USB conference speaker (Jabra Speak, Meeting Owl, or equivalent) placed centrally. Test voice pickup from the far corner of the room. Echos and one-sided audio are the most common failure.
Playback speaker
Virtual attendees' audio must be audible to the full in-room group — not just whoever is seated near the laptop. Use an external speaker at room volume. Test this first.
Assign a dedicated tech host. Someone whose only job is monitoring the video call, managing virtual attendee audio, and flagging when a remote participant is trying to speak. The facilitator cannot do this and run the room simultaneously. This role is non-negotiable for a hybrid session.

Architecture Tour — Virtual Attendees

Virtual attendees skip the walking tour and join Day 1 at 12:30pm, at the start of lunch. The metaphor still works for them — it just needs to be handed to them directly rather than experienced in person.

Send before Day 1

  • The Chicago metaphor reference card (PDF) — same as in-room participants receive on the day
  • The building-by-building table and ISTS parallels (from the Overview section)
  • The observation card prompts — ask virtual attendees to write 3–5 answers before Day 1 starts, based on the metaphor description

During the tour and at lunch

  • Designate one in-room person to photograph 2–3 key moments at each building stop
  • Post photos to the Miro board before 1:00pm — before the warm-up begins
  • At 1:00pm, before the LEGO warm-up begins, spend 5 minutes orienting virtual attendees: one sentence per building and its ISTS parallel
The key is that everyone enters the afternoon sharing the same reference: Pritzker Pavilion as invisible infrastructure that disappears when it works and becomes a crisis when it doesn't. Virtual attendees can hold that metaphor without the walk.

Pre-Tour Briefing

9:00am 30 min Full group

Frame the tour as fieldwork, not sightseeing. Distribute observation cards and sticky notes. Ask everyone to collect at least 5 observations.

Opening framing to read aloud "We are walking through a metaphor. Every building you see today maps to something we are dealing with in ISTS. Collect specific observations — write down what you see, not what you think."

Observation card prompts

  • What makes this city usable for the people who live and work in it?
  • What infrastructure do people rely on but never think about?
  • Where does old and new coexist well — and where does it create friction?
  • What would a first-time visitor find confusing, overwhelming, or invisible?
Hybrid: Virtual attendees skip this session. They receive the metaphor reference card and observation prompts before Day 1 and complete them independently. They join at 12:30pm.

Chicago Icons: Connecting Past and Present

10:00–12:30 ~2 hrs walking 7 stops
Meet: Chicago Architecture Center, 111 E. Wacker Dr.  ·  Format: Walking tour, flat paved route  ·  Access: Wheelchair accessible via alternate routes
BuildingWhat to noticeISTS parallel
Reliance Building 1895Steel-frame construction — invisible, but makes everything after it possibleFoundational infrastructure users and teams depend on without knowing it
City Hall 1911Built to project authority; hard to navigate if you don't know the systemGovernance, approval workflows — necessary but often opaque
Thompson Center 1985Every floor of state government visible from the lobby — radical transparency by design. Workers inside hated it: too hot, ungovernable, impossible to maintain. Now being rebuilt by Google.Systems that signal openness vs. systems that actually work for users. A portal that exposes everything but serves no one.
Marina City 1964"City within a city" — residential, commercial, entertainment in one structureThe ISTS platform ideal: all services discoverable in one place, by anyone who needs them
Daley Plaza / PicassoPublic-facing surface; the sculpture has divided people since 1967Interfaces that require interpretation; what we show vs. what's underneath
Pritzker Pavilion 2004Distributed speaker grid under 36 acres of lawn — invisible but essentialThe closest parallel: hidden services that enable the experience
LondonHouse 1923/20161923 bank building transformed into modern hotel — façade kept, interior rebuiltLegacy modernization: when to preserve vs. rebuild
Pritzker Pavilion is the key metaphor. Most people don't know there's a distributed speaker grid under the lawn across 36 acres. Without it, no one hears the music. That is exactly what ISTS provides — infrastructure that disappears when it works and becomes a crisis when it doesn't.
Thompson Center is the counter-metaphor. Designed to make government transparent and accessible — and experienced by the people inside as hot, ungovernable, and hard to use. Ask the room: which one does our system look like from the outside vs. feel like from the inside?
Tour debrief — use on the walk back or while waiting for lunch (5–10 min, informal) Don't run this as a structured session — let it happen as people walk. Throw out one or two questions and listen. The goal is to surface observations before they evaporate over lunch.
  • Which building stuck with you most — and what did it remind you of in ISTS?
  • Was there a building that made you uncomfortable or that you disagreed with the parallel for?
  • What did you notice that wasn't on the observation card? Something that surprised you.
  • If you had to pick one building that represents where ISTS is today and one that represents where you want it to be — which two would you pick?
  • The Pritzker Pavilion works because you never know it's there. Is there anything in ISTS that works like that right now — and if so, does the team know it?
  • The Thompson Center was designed for citizens and ended up being hated by the workers inside. Where does ISTS do that — something that looks right from the outside and is miserable to maintain from inside?
Hybrid: Virtual attendees skip the tour. Designate someone to photograph 2–3 moments at each stop. Post to Miro before 1:00pm. At 1:00pm, give a 5-minute tour recap to orient virtual attendees before the warm-up begins.

Lunch + Informal Debrief

12:30pm 45 min Unstructured

Play the Jaime Lerner TED Talk ("A Song of the City", ~17 min) while people eat. No framing needed before — just start it. Let the tour be fresh in the room when they watch it. The facilitator listens and takes notes.

Video Jaime Lerner: "A Song of the City" — TED Talk, ~17 min. Lerner transformed Curitiba, Brazil by designing its systems around the people with the least power and the most friction. Bus tube stations with level boarding and pre-pay — because bus riders deserve the dignity of subway riders.
What connects to the tour
  • Invisible infrastructure as the goal — Pritzker Pavilion's speaker grid and Curitiba's BRT both disappear from the user's awareness when they work
  • Constraint forcing better design — Curitiba couldn't afford a subway; Chicago's Reliance Building used steel because masonry couldn't go higher
  • Repurposing vs. rebuilding — Lerner adapted what existed; LondonHouse kept the façade, rebuilt the interior
Where they pull apart
  • Curitiba was designed for the person with the least power. Chicago's icons were mostly designed to project power, commerce, authority — Thompson Center tried for openness but the workers inside it hated it
  • Lerner had a philosophy and executed it deliberately. Chicago's skyline is 130 years of accumulated developer decisions. ISTS probably looks more like Chicago than Curitiba.
Drop this question before people get up "Lerner gave bus riders the same boarding experience as subway riders because he believed they deserved it. Where does ISTS give its users that? And where does it make them feel like the bus rider who has to flag down the driver and count exact change?"
Video debrief — additional discussion questions (pick 1–2, don't run all of them) Use these if the room is energized after the video and there's time before the warm-up. Don't force a structured debrief — these work best as prompts dropped into natural conversation.
  • Lerner didn't build a new city — he redesigned how an existing one worked. What's the equivalent move for ISTS? What already exists that could be redesigned rather than replaced?
  • Lerner started with the people who had the most friction. Who is that in ISTS's user base? Are we designing for them — or for the happy path?
  • The bus tube station works because it removes the decision of "is this the right bus?" — you pay once and the system handles the rest. Where could ISTS remove a decision that users and teams currently have to make?
  • Curitiba's BRT looks simple from the outside. The complexity is hidden in the system design. Is ISTS hiding the right complexity — or are we exposing complexity that users should never have to see?
  • Lerner had a philosophy: design for dignity. What's our equivalent? If you had to write one sentence that explained what ISTS is optimizing for — from a user's perspective — what would it say?

LEGO Warm-Up

1:00pm 60 min Individual builds

Seven phases that move from individual identity to shared values. These values carry into the Dream City build and Day 2. Do not skip any phase — each builds on the last.

1. Build your tower
5 min silent build · any tower you like · use the bricks in front of you
2. Share your tower
Go around the room · one word or phrase about what your tower represents · ~5 min
3. Adapt your tower
What strength or superpower do you bring to this team? Adapt your tower to show it · 7 min build
Note: This is an adaptation — do not deconstruct the tower, add to it or reshape it.
4. Share your superpower
Full circle · 1 min per person · facilitator captures each superpower on the whiteboard · ~15 min
5. Build a team value
Individual build · What principle should guide how this team works? Build it · 8 min silent build
6. Share your value
45 sec per person · facilitator writes each value on the whiteboard · watch for recurring themes · ~12 min
7. Find common ground / Super Story
Which values appeared more than once? Facilitator names 2–3 shared values aloud. Then: everyone positions their value model together to build a shared Super Story — negotiating placement in silence, then discussing. Ask one person: "Tell a story about this team that connects our superpowers and these shared values." · ~8 min
Values carry forward. Write the shared values on a visible surface that stays up for both days. In Session 1 they guide the Dream City build. In Day 2 they anchor prioritization decisions. When someone shares, ask about the model: "Tell us about your model" — not "Tell us about your value."

Value directions for this group

Don't pre-seed specific values — let people bring what they bring. But if someone is stuck, these connect directly to the workshop themes:

Reliability
The Pritzker Pavilion value — earning trust by showing up invisibly, every time
Humility
The Thompson Center lesson — knowing that a system designed to look good from outside can feel ungovernable to the people inside it
Curiosity
The "how do we know this is true?" value — asking before acting
Accountability
The Reliance Building value — owning the steel frame users and teams stand on, whether they know it's there or not
Trust
The Marina City ideal — between ISTS and its users, between teams. The thing that makes a city-within-a-city actually work.
Craft
The LondonHouse value — knowing what to preserve and what to rebuild. Caring about what you hand to users, not just that it functions.

Session 1: Build the Dream City of ISTS

2:10pm 75 min Individual → Landscape
Build Prompt Build the city your customers and users would design if their success were the only measure of success. Step outside your role — you are not an ISTS leader or team member. You are the people we serve. Build their city.

Think across all customer and user groups — developers racing a deadline, employees navigating a new process, business partners integrating a critical system, internal teams trying to move fast, new arrivals who don't yet know where to go. Build experiences, relationships, and outcomes — not systems, platforms, or org charts. If someone builds a portal, ask: "What does it feel like to walk in here?"

Phase 1 — Individual build
20 min · silent · one model per person · before starting, hold one specific person in mind — a real person who depends on ISTS · redirect if someone builds systems instead of experiences
Phase 2 — Share-out
35 min · strict 2-min timebox · visible timer · "Walk us through your city from the perspective of one resident" — every person shares, no exceptions
Phase 3 — Landscape exercise
20 min · mark city edges first · combine in silence · build the missing connections between cities · name the shared landscape · step back and look together
The landscape exercise: Before combining, ask each person to mark where their residents leave their city to get something they need. Then combine in silence. Build roads, bridges, or shared spaces between cities. Name the landscape together. Then ask: Who has to travel farthest? What's missing that no single city has? Where is there a gap no one has filled?

Share-out questions

  • Walk us through your city from the perspective of one resident — who are they and where do they go first?
  • What's the most important building in your city — and why does your customer depend on it?
  • Where does friction disappear in this city? Where does it show up?
  • Is there something your customer needs that doesn't have a building yet?
  • What's the landmark — the thing that makes your city worth living in?
Unmet needs + service gaps (after landscape): "What did multiple people build that we don't fully have yet?" · "Where does the road end — what happens to the customer when our service stops?" · "Which building only works if another building exists?"
Moments of delight: "Where in this city would a customer stop and say, 'I can't believe this exists'?" · "What's the park in your city — the thing that isn't strictly necessary but makes everything feel worth it?" · "What's the one connection between two parts of the city that obviously should exist but doesn't yet?"
Optimistic close: "Everything we built today is a future we're capable of creating. None of it requires inventing something that doesn't exist — it requires deciding what we prioritize for the people who depend on us."
Hybrid: Virtual attendees build their individual model in a Miro frame. For the landscape exercise, they mark their city's edges before combining, then position their frame in the shared canvas and negotiate connections the same as in-room participants.

Session 2: What Stands in the Way?

3:25pm 60 min Individual build → full-circle share-out
Build Prompt You have seen the city you want. Now build the obstacle that stands between here and there.

This is not about who is to blame. Build the structural conditions — organizational, technical, cultural — that prevent the dream city from being built. Be specific: not "communication problems" but the exact moment it breaks.

Phase 1 — Build (10 min · silent)
Everyone builds their own obstacle model — no assignments. Build what you see standing between the dream city and today.
Phase 2 — Share & tag (35 min)
Every person shares their model — 2 min each. After each model, room applies evidence label: We Know, We Believe, or We Don't Know.
Phase 3 — Cluster (15 min)
Room clusters obstacles into 4–6 themes together. These themes become tomorrow's agenda.

Cluster obstacles into

User pain Employee pain Org friction Strategic gap
Apply the evidence test to every obstacle. Write We Know / We Believe / We Don't Know next to each theme in a different color. If most land in "We Believe" and "We Don't Know," name it: "These are the research questions UX can answer."
Naming vs. blaming: If an obstacle model seems to point at a person or team, ask: "What is the structural condition that creates this situation? Can you show that in your model?" This keeps the conversation diagnostic, not political.
Hybrid: Virtual attendees build their obstacle model in a Miro frame. During share-out, they screen-share and narrate. For clustering, the tech host adds their themes to the shared whiteboard alongside in-room themes. Evidence labels applied simultaneously.

Opening & Day 1 Recap

9:00am 15 min Full group

Reground the room in what yesterday uncovered — briefly. The dream city is still visible. The top obstacles are on the wall. Name the top two or three. Then bring back the metaphor before the day begins.

Opening prompt "Yesterday we built the city we want — and we named what stands in the way. Today we go deeper into the people who live in that city, and we figure out where this team can have the biggest impact. Before we start: which part of yesterday's dream city surprised you most — and which obstacle feels most urgent?"
Let 3–4 people answer. This reactivates the shared vocabulary from Day 1 before the first build. You're not looking for a "right" answer — you're reconnecting the room to the metaphor and signaling that the buildings stay in play today.

Research Share-Out

9:15am 60 min Full group

The team shares user research distributed ahead of the offsite. This grounds the room in real evidence before the HMW framing and Design Studio. The first 10 minutes connect findings back to what the room built yesterday — making Day 1 and Day 2 one cohesive arc.

Timing (60 min): 10 min Day 1 bridge → 3 min dot system orientation → 35 min share-outs (~3–4 min per person) → 10 min cluster by theme + Day 1 match → 2 min close and break.
Day 1 Bridge — Open with this (10 min)
Say this before any research is shared — read this exactly "Good morning. Before we get into research: yesterday we built the city we want — and we named what stands in the way. Those obstacles are still on the wall behind you. Here is what we're doing right now: we're going to find out which of those obstacles are confirmed by real users, which ones we only suspected, and which ones we got completely wrong. That is the job of the next hour. Not to validate our opinions — to let the evidence tell us what the city actually needs fixed."
After clustering is complete — say this to connect Day 1 to Day 2 "Now look at your clusters — and look at the Day 1 obstacle wall. Where do they match? Place a dot on any Day 1 obstacle that just got confirmed by user research. Where does a Day 1 obstacle have no corresponding finding? That's either a blind spot from yesterday — or a red-dot gap we haven't researched yet. Either way, it feeds directly into the HMW questions we're writing next."

Physical anchor: Day 1 obstacles stay on their own wall section. Use arrows or string to connect confirmed matches. Unmatched Day 1 obstacles get a red dot — they join the research agenda.

Evidence Tagging — LEGO Dot System (3 min)
Explain before anyone shares — read this exactly "As each finding goes on the wall, we tag it together. Green dot: we know this — confirmed by data, research, or direct observation. Yellow dot: we believe this — strong pattern, not yet formally validated. Red dot: we don't know yet — genuine gap in understanding. Red dots are not bad. They become our research agenda. After each finding I'll ask: We Know, We Believe, or We Don't Know Yet? Quick gut check — tag it, move on. If we're genuinely split, that's a yellow."
Green dot — We know

Confirmed by research, data, or direct observation. This block of the city is mapped.

Yellow dot — We believe

Strong assumption based on experience or pattern — not yet confirmed by evidence.

Red dot — We don't know yet

Gap in understanding. This block is uncharted. Red dots become the research agenda.

If flags are available: green/yellow/red flags planted in the model read well from across the table and photograph clearly — like planning permits or hazard markers in the city.

Share-Out Round (35 min)
Open the round — read this exactly "Let's go around. For each person: tell us who you talked to, one thing that surprised you, and one thing you still don't know after that conversation. About three minutes each. Who wants to start?"
After each finding — say this "Is this a We Know, a We Believe, or a We Don't Know Yet? [hands or call-out] — [color] dot. Up on the wall."

In-the-moment redirects

  • Jumps to solutions: "Hold that — what did you hear the user say? Stay in the finding."
  • Vague finding: "Which user specifically? What exactly couldn't they do?"
  • Evidence debate stalls: "If we're unsure, that's yellow. We can revisit. Keep moving."
  • Dominant voice: Point to a quieter participant: "Before we move on — [name], what did you hear?"
  • At 20-min mark: "About halfway through — one surprise, one gap, tag it, move on."
Clustering + Day 1 Match (10 min)
Step back from the wall — say this "Let's step back. I'm going to read the themes I'm seeing — stop me if you see something different. [Name 3–5 clusters aloud.] Let's move the stickies into clusters. Don't overthink it — if two findings feel related, put them near each other."
After clusters form — connect to Day 1 obstacles "Now look at where the red dots are concentrating. Those are the problems this city doesn't have a map for yet. And look at the Day 1 obstacle wall: where do your clusters match something we named yesterday? Connect the matches. Anything on that wall with no match here either got it wrong — or it's a gap we haven't researched yet."
Before the break — say this "Before we move on: what surprised you most from what you heard this morning? Not from your own conversation — from someone else's."
Take 2–3 responses. Then: "That's why we do the pre-work. You can't get that in a meeting room. The red-dot clusters you just named are what we're turning into design questions next." Give the room a 2-minute break before HMW.

What Is the City Being Asked to Fix?

10:15am 30 min Full group → group formation
Facilitator framing "We just heard the research. Now we're going to turn what we heard into design problems. A How Might We question frames a user problem as an opportunity — specific enough to have a real user and a real pain, but open enough that there's more than one way to solve it."
Timing (30 min): 5 min individual HMW writing (silent, one per sticky) → 10 min share and cluster on wall → 5 min dot vote (2 votes each) → 10 min group formation around top HMW themes.
Individual writing prompt — read this exactly "Working alone and in silence: write one How Might We question per sticky. Pull directly from the research we just heard. Start each one with 'How might we…' One problem per sticky. Aim for 3–5."

Good HMW calibration

  • Too narrow (solves itself): "How might we add a notification in ServiceNow?"
  • Too broad (no direction): "How might we fix the developer experience?"
  • Right: "How might we help an engineer know where their change request is stuck, without having to ask anyone?"

Before they draft — calibrate with this:
  • Too vague: "Developers struggle with tooling." (Which developers? What specifically? Why?)

Team Drafting Guide — work through these in order
1 — Who is the user?

Not a category — a specific person. New hire? Senior engineer on mobile? Security partner? Name them. If your group disagrees, you have two different statements.

2 — What specifically can't they do?

Complete a task? Find information? Get approval? Trust a process? Be as specific as you can. "Struggle with tooling" is not enough — what moment breaks down for them?

3 — Why? (structural cause, not symptom)

Ask "why" twice. "Because the tool is slow" is a symptom. "Because ownership of that tool is split across three teams with no shared roadmap" is a structural cause.

4 — Does it connect to what we and the user value?

Look at the Day 1 team values. Does this problem matter because it cuts against something we — or the user — care about? If so, name it in your statement or keep it in the room as context.

5 — Write the sentence

[User] struggle to [do / understand / access / trust] [something specific] because [root cause].

Read it aloud. If it takes more than one breath, it's too long. If you could read it and not know who to call, it's too vague.

6 — Test it
  • Could someone read this and know who is affected?
  • Could someone read this and know what to research or design next?
  • Is there more than one possible solution — or does it already assume one?
  • Would the user recognize themselves in this statement?
Group drafting (10 min) — say this to start "You have your opportunity area and the drafting guide. Work through the six steps together — in order. Write one clean statement. You have 10 minutes."
Read-aloud round — open each group with this "Read your statement to the room. One sentence. Then pause — let the room sit with it before anyone responds."
Refinement prompts — use if a statement needs work:
  • "Which user specifically? New hire, senior engineer, team lead?"
  • "What specifically can't they do — complete a task, find information, get approval?"
  • "Is the 'because' the real cause, or is it one layer above the real cause?"
  • "Could someone read this and know who to talk to and what to ask? If not — what's missing?"

Closing read-back — do this before the room leaves

Final read — say this exactly "I'm going to read all five statements back to you. Listen for the one that most surprised you — the problem we named today that you've never heard named this clearly before."

Read each statement slowly. Pause between them. After the last one: "These are the problems we're designing from. They leave the room with us today."

Examples

  • Security requirements are experienced as blockers rather than enablers because expectations surface too late in the workflow.
  • New engineers require excessive peer support during onboarding because critical knowledge is distributed and difficult to find.

Introduction to the Method

1:00pm 10 min Full group, before bricks are touched
Say this verbatim "Before we touch any bricks, I want to explain what we're actually doing today — because it probably looks strange from the outside.

LEGO Serious Play is a method for thinking through complex problems by building physical models. Here is the key idea: when you build something with your hands, your brain engages differently than when you talk or write. Ideas that are hard to say out loud become easier to show in a model. And when your model is sitting on the table next to ten other models, the differences in how we each see the same system become visible.

The method has three rules that I'll hold you to throughout both days. One: you build first, then talk. I will give you a prompt — you build silently, then we share. Two: only the person who built the model can say what it means. I will never interpret your model for you, and neither will anyone else. Questions are always directed at the model, not at you. Three: there are no right answers and no wrong models. The tallest tower is not the winner. The most complex build is not the winner. The model that says something true is the one that matters.

We're going to start with a very simple build to get comfortable with the bricks. Then we'll build something real."
Do not skip this. The introduction calibrates the room and prevents the two most common failure modes: people waiting to be told what to build, and people deferring to whoever speaks loudest.

HMW Anatomy — teach this before writing begins

Teaching the Structure — say this before writing
Read this to the room — do not skip it "A good HMW question has one job: make the right solution obvious without naming it. Three parts.

First — the specific user. Not 'users,' not 'engineers.' A real person with a real context. 'A net-new engineer in their first 30 days' is a user. 'Everyone' is not.

Second — the specific action. What can't they do right now? Not a feeling, not a vibe. An action. 'Know where their change request is stuck' is an action. 'Have a better experience' is not.

Third — the structural barrier. The because. This is the most skipped part. It's why the problem exists — a system condition, not a person's fault. 'Because approval status isn't visible outside the ticketing system' is structural. 'Because the process is confusing' is not.

Two traps. Trap one: the solution in disguise. If your HMW starts with 'How might we build…' or 'How might we add…' — stop. You've skipped the problem. Rewind: what can the user not do right now? Trap two: the missing because. If you can't complete 'because [structural condition]' — you don't understand the problem yet. Write it anyway, but give it a red dot."
Calibration — walk through all three before anyone writes
Too narrow — already a solution

"How might we add a ServiceNow notification?"

Only one answer. You've already solved it. Back up: what can the user not do?

Too broad — no user, no direction

"How might we fix developer experience?"

No user. No specific problem. No direction. Anything could go here — that's not a design question.

Right — specific user, specific action, structural barrier

"How might we help an engineer know where their change request is stuck without having to ask a teammate?"

Three different teams could build three completely different solutions — all valid. That's a good HMW.

  • "Is the 'because' the real structural cause, or one layer above it?"
  • "Could someone read this and know who to talk to and what to ask?"

Closing read-back — do this before the room leaves

Final read — say this exactly "I'm going to read all five statements back to you. Listen for the one that most surprised you — the problem we named today that you've never heard named this clearly before."

Read each statement slowly. Pause between them. After the last one: "These are the problems we're designing from. They leave the room with us today."

Facilitation Guide

Running the Room

For the workshop lead and any co-facilitators. Read before the offsite. Bring it with you.

The Facilitator's Core Job

Your job is not to teach, present, or guide people to the right answer. Your job is to:

Protect the process — keep people building and storytelling, not presenting and debating
Surface what's real — ask questions that expose genuine insight, not rehearsed positions
Hold the evidence bar — keep pressing on whether things are known or assumed
Keep the room safe — create conditions where someone can say "I don't actually know our users" without career risk

LSP Method — Three Things to Know

1. Build first, talk second
Every exercise: prompt → quiet build → share-out. Never skip the build. If someone starts talking instead: "Before you tell us — can you show us?"
2. Respond to models, not people
You are asking about the object, not evaluating the person. Always: "Tell us about your model" — never "What did you build?"
3. Silence is building time
You'll feel the urge to fill silence. Don't. If someone is stuck: "Start with one piece that feels right and see where it goes." Then walk away.

Phrases to use vs. avoid

Use"Tell us about your model."
Avoid"What did you build?" (too closed)
Use"What does this piece represent?"
Avoid"That's interesting." (evaluative)
Use"What happens if we remove this?"
Avoid"I like that." (evaluative)
Use"Who else is in this story?"
Avoid"Right." / "Exactly." (signals correct answer)
Pre-Tour Briefing — Success: people leave curious, not briefed. Avoid overloading with LSP theory. Just frame it as fieldwork and let the city do the work.
LEGO Warm-Up — The tower removes the part of the brain that thinks building has to be serious. The superpower phase is where individual energy shows — watch for the person whose superpower surprises everyone. The team value phase is where real content begins: listen carefully and capture every value on the whiteboard, including near-duplicates. The Super Story is the moment the room becomes a team.
Session 1: Dream City — Failure: people build systems and platforms instead of experiences. Redirect mid-build: "What does a developer feel when they arrive here? Can you add that to your model?" In the landscape exercise, position models in silence first — the spatial instinct reveals what verbal discussion hides. Ask: "What is missing from this city?"
Session 2: What Stands in the Way? — The gap-framing protects the room: obstacles between a dream and reality, not complaints about the past. Apply the evidence test to every obstacle. If most land in "we believe" and "we don't know," name it: "These are the research questions UX can answer."
What Is the City Being Asked to Fix? + Build the City You Want Users to Live In — HMW: watch for questions that are too narrow (already a solution) or too broad (no user). Redirect: "Who specifically is the user? What specifically can't they do?" During group formation, watch for everyone clustering into one group — enforce a 4–5 person cap. Design Studio: watch for groups that build systems instead of experiences. Redirect mid-build: "What does the user do first? Show me that moment in the model." The orientation step (reading the HMW aloud and agreeing on the user) prevents the most common failure — groups building three different solutions.
Session 3: Where the City Is Falling Short — Disagreement is the product of this session. Watch for everything clustering toward "high readiness" — apply pressure: "Is this actually ready to act on, or do we wish it were?"
Session 4: What Does the City Need to Get Right for Users by 2027? — If voting is fragmented (everyone votes for their own domain): "Vote for the problem that affects the most people in the worst way — not the problem in your domain."
Session 5: What the City Commits to Change — Push for specificity. If a statement is too broad, ask: "Which user or team? What specifically can't they do? What specifically causes that?" Read statements aloud before the session ends — hearing reveals vagueness that reading doesn't.

The single most important facilitation tool in this workshop. Use it consistently throughout both days — every time someone makes a claim about user behavior, user problems, or organizational challenges.

How to introduce it (Day 1, What Stands in the Way? session) "Every time we identify a problem or insight, we're going to ask: how do we know this is true? We have three categories: we know — evidence, data, or direct research. We believe — strong assumption, no formal evidence. We don't know — a genuine gap. None of these is wrong. 'We don't know' just tells us where we need to do more work."

Sample language

  • "How do we know this is true?"
  • "Is that a 'we know' or a 'we believe'?"
  • "What would we need to see to move this from 'we believe' to 'we know'?"
  • "The fact that we don't know that — is that a problem?"
At the end of Day 2: The "we don't know" bucket is your research agenda. Name it explicitly: "These are not failures. These are the places where UX research can change what ISTS knows about its users before it builds something."
Facilitation Guide

Managing Group Dynamics

The Dominant Voice

One or two people — usually senior leaders — will tend to speak for everyone. Don't ask them to yield. Redirect.

"What does your model say about that?" — directed at someone who hasn't spoken. Physical positioning: stand near the person you want to hear from next.

The Quiet Expert

The person with the most actual user knowledge often says the least. Individual contributor in a room of managers.

"Your model had something the others didn't — can you walk us through this piece again?" Watch for models that are more detailed and grounded than others.

The Skeptic

Someone will think this is silly. Don't argue. Don't convert. Use it.

"Can you build that for me? What does your skepticism look like as a model?" Skeptics almost always engage once their hands are moving.

The Solution-Jumper

"We should just build a unified portal." Usually technically oriented. Skipping the problem to get to the answer.

"Before we get to solutions — can you build me the problem? What does the developer experience look like right now, before the portal exists?"

The Manager Who Speaks for the Team

"Our users all say that…" / "Developers don't really care about…"

Apply the evidence test consistently: "How do we know that? Is this something we have data on, or a strong belief?" Do this without singling anyone out — the room starts applying it to themselves.

The Person Who Won't Share

"Mine is pretty simple / basically the same as what X said."

"Every model gets a voice. What did you build?" Then wait. Do not fill the silence.
